---
title: Points
---

Points are how a delegate raises something outside the normal flow of debate: a procedural
mistake, a personal need, a question. Unlike [[motions|motions]], they aren't voted on; the chair
just rules on them directly.

Of the four described below, only **point of order** exists in the real UN General Assembly. The
other three are Model UN additions, layered on to give delegates more structured ways to
participate and to keep the chair accountable.

## Point of order

Raised when a delegate believes the chair has made a procedural mistake, like skipping roll call
or misapplying a rule. Directed at the chair. Sources genuinely disagree on
whether it can interrupt a speaker mid-speech: some conferences allow it "within reason" for
serious errors, others reserve interrupting power for personal privilege only. Don't assume either
way going in.

## Point of personal privilege

For a delegate's own ability to participate: can't hear, the room's too cold, a broken
placard, audio problems. This is the point most consistently described as allowed to interrupt a
speaker, and even then, typically only when the issue genuinely can't wait (inaudibility being the
classic example).

## Point of parliamentary inquiry

A question to the chair about procedure itself, like "what happens if this motion fails" or "how
much time is left in this caucus." It's about the rules, not about debate content, and it cannot
interrupt a speaker.

## Point of information

A question directed at a delegate, not the chair, about a speech they just gave, used during
[[speeches-and-the-gsl|GSL]] speeches when the speaker has yielded to questions. The distinction
from parliamentary inquiry is both who it's directed at (a delegate, not the chair) and what it's
about (the content of a speech, not a procedural rule).

Some conferences merge parliamentary inquiry and point of information under a single label, or use
"point of information to the chair" for what others call parliamentary inquiry. If a term feels
like it's being used slightly differently than described here, that's likely why.
