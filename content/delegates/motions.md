---
title: Motions
---

A motion is how a delegate proposes to change what the committee is doing: move from the GSL
into a caucus, close debate, bring a resolution to the floor. Anyone can raise one; the committee
decides whether to adopt it, usually by simple majority.

Worth knowing up front: motions, seconding, and yielding don't exist in the real UN General
Assembly. They're Model UN's own parliamentary scaffolding, built to give a room full of students
a fair, orderly way to run debate, not an attempt at UN authenticity for its own sake.

## Motion to extend

Normally, this is only said before the opening speech starts to increase the allocated time for opening speeches (usually 1 minute). To lets say, 90 seconds or 75 seconds. This is useful when your opening speech is too long (hopefully it is since 1 minute is not enough to cover most topics) and is commonly used in most sessions. 

## Moderated caucus

Chair-controlled and structured. The GSL is suspended, and the chair calls on delegates one at a
time, usually by raised placard, to speak briefly, often on a specific sub-topic named in the
motion. A moderated caucus motion states both the **total time** and the **per-speaker time**
(e.g. "10 minutes, 30 seconds per speaker"). The clock only runs while someone is actually
speaking.

## Unmoderated caucus

Informal and unstructured. Delegates leave their seats and negotiate freely for the motioned
duration, with no chair-mediated turn-taking. The motion only needs a total time, not a topic.
This is genuinely where most of the real work happens: bloc-building, negotiation, and the actual
drafting of [[working-papers-and-resolutions|working papers]]. See [[caucusing]] for what this
looks like in practice.

## Closing or extending debate

A motion to **close debate** ends discussion on the current topic and moves the committee
straight into [[voting-procedure|voting]]. A motion to **extend debate** lengthens the current
debate or speaking time instead of ending it. The vote threshold to close debate is one of the
more conference-variable numbers. You'll see both simple majority and two-thirds cited, so
don't assume either without checking your conference's rules.

## Introducing a draft resolution

Once a draft resolution has met its sponsor/signatory requirements (see
[[working-papers-and-resolutions|working papers and resolutions]]), a delegate moves to introduce
it, bringing it to the floor to be read and debated.

## Withdrawing sponsorship

A sponsor can withdraw from a resolution at any time via a written motion to the dais. Useful if
your delegation's position shifts and you no longer want your name attached to it.

## The "table" trap

If you hear "motion to table the resolution," check which convention your conference uses before
you assume you know what it means. In **American** usage, tabling sets a motion aside, and it kills
momentum, sometimes permanently. In **British/Commonwealth** usage, tabling means the opposite:
bringing it forward for consideration. Conferences run by either tradition exist, and the same
phrase means opposite things.

## When multiple motions are on the floor

Which motion the chair takes first when several are raised at once isn't standardized. A common
pattern is that the most disruptive motion gets voted on first. ( and unmoderated caucus > consultation of the whole > moderated caucus > GSL with GSL being the least disruptive and unmoderated caucus being the most disruptive. If there are multiple same motions the one with the longest time is considered the most disruptive of the same time ),
but exact precedence rules genuinely differ by conference.
