---
title: Working papers and resolutions
---

Most resolutions start as scratch notes during an [[caucusing|unmoderated caucus]] and go through
a couple of clear stages before they're something the whole committee debates.

## Working paper → draft resolution

A **working paper** is the informal starting point — a collaborative draft with none of a
resolution's formatting requirements yet, and it isn't debated on the floor in this form.

To become a **draft resolution**, it needs to clear two things:

1. **Enough sponsors and/or signatories.** The usual benchmark cited is roughly a fifth of the
   committee, though the exact split (say, 5 sponsors plus 15 signatories, vs. some other
   breakdown) is set by the conference, not fixed across all of MUN.
2. **Approval of form** from the dais — the chair checking it's structured correctly before it can
   be introduced.

## Sponsors vs. signatories

- **Sponsors** wrote it and substantively agree with it. At most conferences, only sponsors can
  formally introduce the draft, and only sponsors can accept a [[amendments|friendly amendment]]
  to it. A sponsor generally can't vote against their own resolution without formally withdrawing
  first — some conferences don't even allow withdrawal once debate has started.
- **Signatories** aren't endorsing the content — they just want to see it debated. A signatory can
  vote against the resolution they signed onto without any contradiction.

Once a draft resolution is formally introduced, it stops being just the sponsors' document — the
committee as a whole can propose changes to it from that point on.

## How a resolution is structured

Two clause types, each with its own grammar convention:

- **Preambulatory clauses** set up context — why the issue matters, what's already been tried.
  They start with a capitalized present participle ("*Recalling*," "*Noting with concern*,"
  "*Emphasizing*") and end in a comma.
- **Operative clauses** state what the committee is actually deciding or calling for. They start
  with a capitalized present-tense verb ("*Calls upon*," "*Urges*," "*Decides*"), and each ends
  with a semicolon — except the very last, which ends with a period.

This mirrors how the real UN structures its own resolutions, though the sponsor/signatory
threshold system most conferences use to gate introduction is a Model UN addition — a real UN
draft resolution is typically tabled by one lead sponsor after informal consultation with other
member states, without a fixed numeric co-sponsor requirement.
